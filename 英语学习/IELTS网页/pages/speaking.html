PART 1 · INTRODUCTION

P1 · 30+ 高频话题范答

PEEL 万能框架

  • Point — 直接回答
  • Explanation — 解释 / 说明
  • Example — 举一个具体例子
  • Link — 简单收尾

1. Q: Do you work or study?

A: I'm actually doing a mix of both at the moment. I'm in my final year studying data science at university, and I've also been doing a part-time internship at a fintech startup since last March. It's been pretty intense, juggling lectures with about twenty hours of work each week, but I'm enjoying the variety.

2. Q: Why did you choose this subject?

A: Honestly, it was a mix of curiosity and pragmatism. I'd been fascinated by statistics in high school, and the rise of AI made it look like a field that wasn't going to slow down anytime soon. Plus, I had a teacher who used to run little coding clubs after school — I think she planted the seed.

3. Q: Where do you come from?

A: I'm originally from Chengdu, in southwestern China. It's a medium-sized city — by Chinese standards, anyway — best known for its food and its pandas. I moved away for university but go back whenever the holidays allow.

4. Q: Do you like your hometown?

A: Yes, very much. It has this rare combination of being big enough to have everything I need — good cafés, decent museums, a proper public transport system — without feeling overwhelming. The food alone is reason enough to love the place.

5. Q: Has your hometown changed much in recent years?

A: Massively. When I was a kid, half the city was farmland or low-rise housing. Now there's a brand-new metro line, several skyscraper districts, and entire neighbourhoods I don't even recognise. Some of it is exciting; some of it I miss.

6. Q: Do you live in a house or a flat?

A: I live in a flat — a small two-bedroom one I share with a friend. It's on the eighth floor of a residential building, so we get pretty decent natural light, which I appreciate.

7. Q: What is your favourite room?

A: Probably the living room. It's where the sofa is — and most importantly the bookshelves — so it's both a relaxing space and where I do most of my reading. We've got a big window facing east, so morning light pours in.

8. Q: What's your favourite food?

A: I'd have to say spicy hotpot, which is a Sichuan classic. It's interactive, communal, and arguably the most fun way to eat. Plus the variety is unmatched — you can put almost anything in there.

9. Q: Do you cook?

A: A bit — nothing too ambitious. I usually stick to about five or six dishes I can cook on autopilot: stir-fried vegetables, simple pasta, the occasional curry. Cooking properly takes time I don't always have.

10. Q: Have your eating habits changed?

A: Definitely. As a teenager I'd happily live on fried food and bubble tea; now I find myself thinking about vegetables and protein content, which is mildly horrifying. I think it's a combination of slower metabolism and just paying more attention.

11. Q: Do you have any hobbies?

A: Reading is the big one — I get through maybe two books a month. I've also picked up photography in the past year, mostly street photography on weekends. Both give me a way to switch off from screens.

12. Q: What do you do at weekends?

A: Saturdays are usually busier — I might meet friends for brunch, do my groceries, maybe catch a film. Sundays I deliberately keep low-key: reading, a long walk, planning the week ahead.

13. Q: Do you read books?

A: Quite a lot, actually. I lean towards non-fiction — history and behavioural economics are favourites — but I always have a novel going as well, just to balance things out. Last month I read Sapiens again; it's one of those books I revisit every couple of years.

14. Q: Paper books or e-books?

A: Mostly paper, honestly — there's something about flipping pages and the physicality of a real book. That said, I use a Kindle for travelling, since carrying three books in a backpack defeats the purpose.

15. Q: Do you listen to music?

A: Daily. I treat music almost like background colour — different playlists for different moods. When I'm working I lean towards classical or instrumental; when I'm cooking it tends to be something with more energy.

16. Q: What kind of music do you like?

A: I've gradually shifted towards quieter genres — indie folk, neoclassical, that sort of thing. As a teenager I was deep into pop and hip-hop, but my taste seems to be slowly drifting into the background.

17. Q: Do you play any instrument?

A: I played piano for about eight years as a kid, although I wouldn't claim to be any good now. I still sit down and play occasionally — usually badly, and only when no one's listening.

18. Q: Do you like sports?

A: I'm more of a participant than a fan — I'd rather play badly than watch professionally. I run two or three times a week, and I'll play badminton with friends when the weather's right.

19. Q: Did you do sports when you were younger?

A: Yes, quite a lot. Primary school I was obsessed with table tennis; in high school I switched to basketball. Looking back, those sessions probably did more for my friendships than for my actual skill.

20. Q: Do you like travelling?

A: Love it — though I'm pickier than I used to be. I now prefer slower, longer stays in one place over rushing through five cities in a week. There's just more to see when you're not constantly packing.

21. Q: What's the weather like in your hometown?

A: Pretty mild overall, but quite humid. Summers can be muggy — temperatures hovering around 30°C with a lot of cloud cover. Winters are short and not too cold, although the lack of sun gets to me sometimes.

22. Q: Do you use the internet a lot?

A: Probably more than I should — somewhere between five and seven hours a day, between work, study and entertainment. I've been trying to cut back, mostly by deleting one or two apps I genuinely don't miss.

23. Q: Do you watch films?

A: Maybe one or two a week. I'm drawn to slower-paced cinema — Asian arthouse, Scandinavian dramas — but I also enjoy a good blockbuster once in a while. Variety is part of the fun.

24. Q: Do you take photos often?

A: Yes, almost daily, although most are just phone shots — meals, sunsets, things I want to remember. On weekends I'll sometimes take my proper camera out for a longer walk.

25. Q: Do you like shopping?

A: It depends on what for. Groceries — yes, surprisingly relaxing. Clothes — not really; I tend to decide on something quickly and leave. Browsing for fun isn't quite my thing.

26. Q: What do you usually do in the morning?

A: I'm not a morning person, but I'm trying to be. I start with a glass of water, a quick stretch, then breakfast while listening to a news podcast. By 8:30 I'm at my desk.

27. Q: Do you keep in touch with your friends from school?

A: A handful, yes — maybe four or five from primary and middle school combined. We have a group chat that comes alive about once a month, and we try to meet up at least once a year over Spring Festival.

28. Q: Do you like writing letters or emails?

A: I write emails constantly for work, but I find the experience pretty mechanical. Handwritten letters — those I love, but I almost never write them. Once or twice a year for birthdays, perhaps.

29. Q: Do you remember a special gift you received?

A: Yes — my grandmother gave me a fountain pen for my eighteenth birthday. It's nothing extravagant, but it had been hers, and the small inscription she added means a lot. I still use it for important notes.

30. Q: Do you prefer mornings or evenings?

A: Evenings, for sure. My energy and focus peak somewhere around 9 PM, which is inconveniently late for most schedules. I've made peace with the fact that I'll never be a 5 AM-runner type.

PART 2 · CUE CARDS

P2 · 10 个完整范例

三段式结构

i.

What (30 秒)

是什么 / 是谁 / 在哪 / 什么时候。

ii.

How (60 秒)

具体过程或细节。讲故事、给细节。

iii.

Why / Feeling (30 秒)

为什么重要 / 让你有什么感受。

1 分钟准备时间该做的事

  1. 在草稿纸上写下 5–6 个关键词（不要写完整句）。
  2. 在脑中确认 What / How / Why-feeling 三段如何展开。
  3. 选一个具体的、最容易展开的例子。

卡片 1 · 人物类

Describe a person you admire. You should say:

  • who this person is
  • how you know them
  • what they have achieved
  • and explain why you admire them.

[What — 30s] The person I'd most like to talk about is my undergraduate supervisor, Dr Liu — a quietly remarkable woman who runs a research group on environmental data at my university. I first met her about three years ago, when I joined her group as a second-year undergraduate.

[How — 60s] Her work focuses on tracking groundwater contamination across rural China — not glamorous, but enormously consequential. What's impressed me most is the way she combines technical depth with patient mentorship. She publishes in top international journals, yet still spends two or three afternoons a week sitting with junior students, going through their code line by line. I remember one Saturday when I was struggling with a particular dataset; she sat with me for almost four hours, explaining not just the answer but how to think about the problem. And she always insists on first authorship for the students who do the actual work — even when convention would let her take credit. In an academic culture that often rewards self-promotion, that kind of generosity stands out.

[Why-feeling — 30s] What I really admire is that she's quietly principled in a profession that doesn't always reward it. She's shown me that you can be both rigorous and kind, both ambitious and generous. I think about her example often when I'm tempted to cut corners — which is the highest compliment I can give a mentor.

卡片 2 · 地点类

Describe a quiet place you like to go. You should say:

  • where it is
  • how often you go there
  • what you do there
  • and explain why you like it.

[What — 30s] One of my favourite quiet spots is a small Japanese-style garden tucked behind the city library — it's barely fifteen minutes from my flat, but most people walk past without realising it exists.

[How — 60s] The garden is no bigger than a tennis court, but every square metre is carefully composed: there's a small koi pond at the centre, surrounded by mossy stones, with a few maple trees that turn deep red every November. There's a wooden bench near the back wall — that's my regular spot. I usually go on Sunday mornings, often with a book and a thermos of tea, and I'll stay for an hour or two without speaking to anyone. What's nice is the level of attention to detail — the gardener rakes the gravel in different patterns each week, and the seasonal flowers rotate slowly. Even the sound of the small fountain is calibrated to be just loud enough to mask traffic. It feels deliberately designed for slowness, which is rare in a city my size.

[Why-feeling — 30s] What I appreciate most is that it gives me permission to do nothing. In a world where everything optimises for productivity, having a place that just lets you sit and look at moss is genuinely restorative. I always leave feeling like time has slowed down.

卡片 3 · 物品类

Describe a gift you received that was important to you. You should say:

  • what it was
  • who gave it to you
  • why they gave it to you
  • and explain why it was important.

[What — 30s] The gift that immediately comes to mind is a vintage Parker fountain pen that my grandmother gave me on my eighteenth birthday. It came in a small wooden case with a handwritten note tucked under the lid.

[How — 60s] The pen itself wasn't expensive — it was an older model from the 1970s that she'd kept in a drawer for years. My grandmother had been a literature teacher, and she'd used this very pen to mark essays for nearly two decades. What made it special was the inscription she'd had engraved on the cap: a short line from a poem she used to read to me when I was little. She also tucked in a letter — three handwritten pages — explaining why she chose that particular pen and what she hoped I'd do with it. Reading it nearly made me cry. She passed away two years later, so it became even more meaningful in retrospect. I still use the pen for important notes — birthday cards, my own letters to friends — and it always feels like she's quietly there.

[Why-feeling — 30s] What made the gift important wasn't its monetary value but the care and attention she put into choosing something so personal. It reminded me that the best gifts carry a story. It's also become a kind of compass — I try to give gifts the same way now.

卡片 4 · 事件类

Describe a time when you helped someone. You should say:

  • who you helped
  • how you helped them
  • why they needed help
  • and explain how you felt about helping them.

[What — 30s] About a year ago, a younger student at university — her name is Wei — was preparing for her final-year project on machine learning, and she was completely overwhelmed. She asked me for help, and we ended up working together over several weekends.

[How — 60s] Wei had chosen an ambitious topic — using neural networks for medical image classification — but she'd never actually trained a model from scratch. The deadline was three weeks away, and she was on the verge of changing topics entirely. I'd done something similar in my second year, so I offered to mentor her informally. We met every Saturday for about a month, three or four hours at a time, in the library. I didn't write any code for her — that would have defeated the purpose — but I helped her break the problem down into smaller pieces, debug her models, and figure out which papers were actually relevant. By the third week she was running experiments on her own, and her final defence went really well — she got a distinction.

[Why-feeling — 30s] Honestly, I got more out of it than I expected. Explaining things forced me to understand them better myself, and watching her gain confidence was strangely rewarding. It made me realise that helping isn't a one-way transaction; you almost always learn something in return.

卡片 5 · 媒体类

Describe a book that changed your view of something. You should say:

  • what the book was
  • when you read it
  • what it was about
  • and explain how it changed your view.

[What — 30s] The book that immediately comes to mind is Sapiens by Yuval Noah Harari. I read it during my second year of university, around three years ago, when I was looking for something that would broaden my view of history.

[How — 60s] The book traces the entire arc of human history, from the cognitive revolution onwards. What struck me most was how it framed "money", "religion" and even "nations" as shared stories rather than physical things. There's a chapter where Harari argues that our ability to believe in collective fictions is what allowed us to cooperate at scale — much more than any biological advantage. I remember underlining passage after passage; I'd never thought about civilisation that way. The chapter on agriculture was the one that really shifted my thinking — Harari calls it "history's biggest fraud", because while it allowed bigger populations, it also made individual lives harder. That's a kind of inversion of the standard narrative I'd been taught all my life.

[Why-feeling — 30s] What changed for me was that I started seeing modern institutions — schools, companies, even social media — as evolving narratives, not as fixed structures. That shift has made me a more curious and slightly more skeptical reader in general, which is why this book holds a special place for me.

卡片 6 · 经历类

Describe a skill you have learned recently. You should say:

  • what the skill is
  • when and where you learned it
  • how you learned it
  • and explain why you wanted to learn it.

[What — 30s] One skill I've been picking up over the past six months is film photography — specifically, shooting on a manual 35mm camera. It's been a hobby project entirely outside of my main field of study.

[How — 60s] I bought a second-hand Pentax K1000 at a flea market last spring for about 400 yuan. It's completely manual — no autofocus, no light meter readouts, just three dials. The learning curve was steeper than I expected. I started by reading the manual, watching YouTube tutorials and shooting test rolls of cheap film just to understand exposure. The first few rolls were almost entirely useless — too dark, out of focus, sometimes I'd forgotten to advance the film. But by month three I was starting to get one or two keepers per roll, and by month six I was actively choosing shots. The best moment was when a photo I took of my grandmother actually came out beautifully — sharp, well-composed, with the kind of soft grain you only get from film. I had it framed and gave it to her.

[Why-feeling — 30s] What drew me to film was the slowness — with only 36 frames, you actually have to think before each shot. It's a useful counterweight to a life lived mostly through screens, and it's taught me to pay attention in a way that digital never did.

卡片 7 · 经历类

Describe a journey you took by public transport.

Story line: 大学毕业前一次和朋友的从北京到成都的火车旅行（32 小时硬卧）。

What: The longest train journey I've taken — 32 hours from Beijing to Chengdu in a hard sleeper carriage, with three friends, just after our undergraduate graduation in 2024.

How: We had the bottom four bunks of a six-person compartment. We brought books, cards, and far too many snacks — sunflower seeds, instant noodles, dumplings someone had homemade. The first ten hours were exciting; we played card games, talked loudly. The middle stretch was hard — somewhere outside Xi'an, the carriage felt very small, the air felt very stale. But the second night, lying in our bunks watching the unfamiliar countryside go past with a Bluetooth speaker playing softly, it became something memorable. We arrived exhausted but somehow closer than we'd been before.

Why-feeling: What made the trip special wasn't the destination but the compressed time together. You don't usually get 32 uninterrupted hours with friends; there's nowhere else to be. It's the kind of journey that's worth doing once, mostly so you understand why a flight exists.

卡片 8 · 物品类

Describe a useful app on your phone.

What: Notion — a note-taking and project management app I've been using daily for about two years.

How: I use it for everything: lecture notes, reading lists, a personal database of articles I want to remember, weekly planning. The thing I love most is that it's flexible — pages can be databases, calendars, kanban boards, or just plain text. I have a "reading log" where every book I've finished sits with a short summary and a rating; a "weekly review" page; a "interesting people I've met" list. It's basically become my second brain.

Why-feeling: What I appreciate is that the app stays out of the way. Many productivity apps demand you to maintain them; Notion mostly does what you want it to. It's made me significantly more organised — or at least, given me the illusion of being organised, which might be the same thing.

卡片 9 · 人物类

Describe a creative person you know.

What: My cousin Mei — a part-time illustrator who runs a small Etsy shop selling watercolour bookplates and bookmarks.

How: Mei is two years older than me. By day she works at a translation agency, but in evenings and weekends she paints — usually small, intricate watercolours of birds, leaves, and tiny scenes from old Chinese novels. She started selling them online about three years ago, and now ships her work to customers in Europe and North America. What's amazing is her consistency — she paints almost every day, even if just for thirty minutes, and she keeps a sketchbook log going for years now. She also teaches an online watercolour class on weekends, with about twenty students.

Why-feeling: What I admire is the quiet discipline. She's not famous, doesn't post much on social media, but has built something genuinely beautiful through small daily acts. It's a useful counter-example to the culture of overnight success.

卡片 10 · 事件类

Describe an important decision you made.

What: The decision, made in my second year, to switch my major from computer science to data science — a related but distinct field.

How: The switch involved transferring departments, retaking a couple of core modules, and adding an extra semester to my degree. It wasn't an easy decision. I'd done well in pure computer science, my classmates were great, and there was the obvious cost of an additional semester. What pushed me was a summer internship at a healthcare startup — I'd spent two months building data pipelines and statistical models, and realised I cared much more about the analytical side than about, say, system architecture or compilers. I spoke to two professors, took a week to think, then went to the registrar in October 2023.

Why-feeling: Looking back, it was the right call. I'm now finishing my degree with both technical depth and a much clearer sense of what kind of work I want to do. The bigger lesson, though, was that sunk costs don't matter — the right question isn't "what have I already invested" but "where do I actually want to be".

9 大 P3 抽象话题 + 示范

1. 关于阅读 / 媒介转变

Q: Is reading becoming less popular among young people?

A: I think it depends on what we count as "reading". If we mean printed novels, then yes — the numbers have dropped, and young people spend more time on short-form content like TikTok or news feeds. But if we broaden the definition to include articles, blogs, even subtitles, they're arguably reading more than any previous generation. So I'd say the format has shifted, not the activity itself.

2. 关于教育 / 在线学习

Q: Is online learning as effective as classroom learning?

A: Probably not, for most students — though it depends heavily on the subject and the student. For self-directed learners and for skills like programming, online courses can be just as good or better, because you can move at your own pace. But for younger students or for collaborative subjects, the lack of in-person feedback and peer pressure makes a real difference. The pandemic gave us a natural experiment, and most countries reported a drop in learning outcomes. So online is a useful supplement, but not yet a full replacement.

3. 关于科技 / AI

Q: Will artificial intelligence replace many jobs?

A: Yes, but probably in a more nuanced way than the headlines suggest. AI is very good at narrow, repetitive cognitive tasks — drafting boilerplate emails, basic image classification, simple legal review. So roles built around those tasks will shrink. But for most jobs, AI will be a tool that changes how people work rather than eliminating them entirely. A radiologist will probably keep their job but will work alongside AI that triages scans. The question isn't really "Will AI replace humans?" but "Will humans who use AI replace humans who don't?"

4. 关于环境 / 责任

Q: Whose responsibility is it to protect the environment — individuals, companies, or governments?

A: Honestly, all three, but in very different proportions. The most impactful changes have to come from governments — through regulation, taxation and infrastructure choices — and from companies, who control most production and supply chains. Individual action matters too, but it's mostly symbolic at the scale we need; even if every household recycled perfectly, it wouldn't undo industrial-scale emissions. So I think the framing of "personal responsibility" can actually be a distraction from the systemic changes that are really needed.

5. 关于工作 / 平衡

Q: Is work-life balance more important than salary?

A: For most people, eventually yes — but it varies by life stage. In your twenties, you might reasonably prioritise income and career growth, because the compounding effect on your future is significant. By your thirties and beyond, with family responsibilities, declining energy, and clearer values, balance becomes more important. So the answer is less "balance is always more important" and more "your priorities should evolve, and you should be honest with yourself about where you are."

6. 关于全球化 / 文化

Q: Is globalisation a threat to local cultures?

A: It's both a threat and an opportunity, and which one dominates depends on the culture. The threat is real — global brands, global media, global English have flattened many local traditions, especially for languages and cuisines. But globalisation has also created new audiences for previously obscure local cultures; K-pop and Japanese anime are arguably more globally influential than anyone would have predicted twenty years ago. So the picture is more complex than simply "global swallows local".

7. 关于城市 / 生活

Q: Is life in big cities better than in small towns?

A: Better is the wrong word — they offer different things. Big cities give you more opportunities, more diversity, more anonymity. Small towns give you community, slower pace, cheaper living. For ambitious young professionals, cities tend to win. For families and retirees, small towns often work better. The rise of remote work has actually opened up a third option — small-town living with city-level work, which a lot of my friends are now trying.

8. 关于科技 / 隐私

Q: Is too much information being collected about us online?

A: Yes, and most users don't even know the scale. Every click, scroll, and pause is recorded somewhere — often by companies most users have never heard of. Worse, this data is bought, sold and aggregated, so even "anonymous" data can be re-identified surprisingly easily. I think this will become one of the big political issues of the next decade, and we'll look back on the current era as the wild west of digital privacy — a regulatory void that we eventually filled. Europe's GDPR was a first attempt, but most of the world has barely started.

9. 关于幸福 / 价值观

Q: Does money bring happiness?

A: Up to a point, definitely. Research consistently shows that income improves wellbeing up to about a basic level of comfort — roughly enough to not worry about rent, food and healthcare. Beyond that, the link weakens significantly. Above that threshold, happiness depends more on relationships, purpose, and physical health than on additional money. So the answer is — yes, until it doesn't.

§ VII · MISTAKES TO AVOID

5 + 5 个口语降分陷阱

  1. 长停顿。想不出例子时用 "Let me give you an example..." / "What comes to mind is..." 拖一秒，比真的沉默 5 秒强很多。
  2. 万能模板感。 "There are advantages and disadvantages..." 这种考官一听就反感。换成更生活化的 "Honestly, it cuts both ways for me..."
  3. 背套话。不要硬塞 idioms。用错比不用扣分还重。
  4. 过度纠正自己。说错就继续。来回改 3 遍反而影响 fluency。
  5. 音量太小 / 太平。抑扬顿挫和音量本身就是 pronunciation 评分的一部分。
  6. 只用最简单句。一句完一句不嵌套，Grammatical Range 拿不到 6 分。
  7. 填充词太多。"um" / "uh" / "like" 一句一个，听感很差。
  8. 不举具体例子。"Some people..." / "Many things..." 这种笼统说法等于没说。
  9. 不眨眼地说太快。追求快不代表流利。考官要听清楚，而不是听到一堆乱碎词。
  10. 背范例答案被识破。考官每天听 20+ 考生，识别"背诵"的能力远超你想象。重点不在用什么词，而在组织能力。
口语高分的本质：把英语当工具用，而不是当作"表演"。 — 一位前考官的建议
